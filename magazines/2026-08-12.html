Front page · Macro · Markets
Prices cooled in July. The argument inside the Fed is now about putting rates up, not bringing them down.

Inflation slows to 3.4%, and the Fed still has its finger near the trigger.

Annual US inflation dipped to 3.4% in July as food and fuel costs slowed, with housing keeping the headline stickier than the Fed would like. The New York Times read is blunter than the number: benign data buys the Fed time, but the live debate is whether to raise, and that debate is not settled.

The reason sits in the Gulf. The war with Iran has whipped oil around all quarter, and Gulf exporters are now spending billions building routes that avoid the Strait of Hormuz entirely. Andy Burnham has already warned that the conflict could dent UK growth next year through fuel prices and supply chains.

What it means for your day Any 2027 client plan built on falling rates and calm freight is now built on sand. If a Tolani deck has a cost-of-goods assumption in it, put a sensitivity line under it this week. For ShelfBuddy, expect brand partners to squeeze trade spend before they squeeze anything else, which is exactly when a cheaper route to shelf gets a hearing.

# what happened
Tailscale chased a run of database corruption across its own fleet and landed on a write-ahead-log reset bug in SQLite that has been in the code for roughly sixteen years. Not a race in their application. Not a bad disk. The database itself.

# why you should care
SQLite is the most widely deployed database on the planet. It is under your phone, your browser, most of your desktop apps, and a growing slice of edge and serverless tooling. A quiet corruption path is exactly the sort of thing that produces the bug nobody can reproduce and everybody blames on the user.

# the transferable lesson
"It has been in production for years" is not evidence of correctness. It is evidence that nobody looked. The same applies to your own quiet infrastructure: the digest build, the outreach scripts, the kanban sync. A backup you have never restored is not a backup, it is a hope.

The deepest eclipse Britain has seen since 1999 happens over your head today.

Reported by the BBC and the New York Times, 12 August 2026

Up to 96 per cent of the Sun will be covered by the Moon over parts of the UK today, the deepest partial eclipse these islands have had since August 1999. Spain, Portugal, Iceland, Greenland and parts of Russia sit under the path of totality, where the sky goes properly dark in the middle of the day.

Cloud is the only real enemy and the Scottish forecast is already grim. If the weather closes in, someone has built an aggregator of live webcams along the totality path and parked it on the Hacker News front page, which is the sensible fallback. In Spain, one astrophysicist went home to her remote village to run an impromptu science class and found several hundred ravers had chosen the same field.

Then keep tonight free as well. The Perseids peak this evening into tomorrow morning, on a moonless sky the night after a solar eclipse. Two proper celestial events inside eighteen hours is not a normal Wednesday.

One rule, and one for the boys Do not look at a partial eclipse without certified eclipse glasses, and do not point a phone camera at the Sun unless you fancy buying a new sensor. Pinhole projection through a cereal box costs nothing, works perfectly, and is the sort of thing children remember for thirty years.

05

Someone is scanning the internet wearing ClaudeBot's face.

Known Agents has published evidence of mass vulnerability scanning where the traffic spoofs the user agents of AI crawlers, ClaudeBot among them. If your site allows or blocks by user agent string alone, you are now making security decisions based on a line of text that anybody can type.

It was that sort of morning. GitHub declared an incident on Pull Requests and Issues, so if a push looked broken today it probably was. Researchers demonstrated a device hijack through Zoom screen sharing, and a public AI tool found the flaw in under twenty prompts. On the better side, Chrome has shipped device-bound session credentials, the strongest defence yet against stolen-cookie account takeover.

Do these four things today
  • Verify crawler traffic on the Lucky Goods Club and ShelfBuddy sites by reverse DNS or published IP range, never by user agent string
  • Scan Netlify and Cloudflare logs for scan bursts claiming to be ClaudeBot or GPTBot, and rate-limit the pattern rather than the name
  • Update Chrome everywhere so device-bound session credentials are actually switched on
  • If any client shares price-sensitive material over Zoom screen share, forward the advisory before their next board call

Pricing

Nobody knows how to charge for AI. Nobody knows how to budget for it either.

For You
What's happening

The BBC's piece on tokenomics lands on the problem you met the hard way in July. Buyers cannot tell what a job will cost before they run it. Sellers cannot price a product whose unit cost moves with model choice, context length, and how talkative the agent decides to be that afternoon.

So vendors drift from per-seat to per-token to outcome pricing and back again, and buyers respond by capping spend, batching work, and pushing mechanical tasks to cheaper models. Which is the correct response, and almost nobody has written it down.

There is a consulting product hiding in here. FMCG marketing teams are signing AI content tools with no unit economics and no ceiling. A one-page audit answering "what does this actually cost us per asset" would sell tomorrow, and you have the scar tissue to write it.

An AI wrote sixteen viruses that had never existed. All sixteen worked.

Scientists trained a model on libraries of DNA, then asked it to write recipes for viral genomes. Sixteen of the results were viable and produced real, functioning viruses found nowhere in nature. They target bacteria, which is the reassuring half of the sentence. The method is the other half.

Read it alongside the rest of the week's field notes. Capybaras wandered into a state legislature in Mato Grosso and, by all accounts, conducted themselves better than the members. A separate study argues that faeces fuelled the burst of complexity in the Cambrian. Nature has not lost its sense of humour.

10.
The most argued-over post on Hacker News today, 395 points and 329 comments, is a single sentence that anyone running a services business should read as a warning shot:

"AI is removing the middle class of software engineering."

For You

The argument runs like this. Juniors were cheap and are being absorbed by tooling. Seniors are scarce and are getting more valuable. The comfortable middle, the competent implementer who turned a clear brief into working output, is squeezed from both ends. Three hundred and twenty-nine comments, and most of them argue about how fast, not whether.

Swap "software engineering" for "marketing services" and the sentence survives intact. The middle of your market is the agency that takes a clear brief and returns competent execution. That is exactly the layer Thrive Holdings just raised two billion dollars to buy and automate, page 09.

The two defensible ends are the two you already own. Judgement at the top: seventeen years of knowing which FMCG launches fail and why, which no model has lived through. Product at the bottom: ShelfBuddy, where the work is done once and sold many times. Anything in between that bills by the hour for competent execution is on borrowed time. Price the judgement, ship the product, and stop quoting for the middle.
