The American household finally flinched. Every FMCG plan for 2027 was written before this print landed.

US retail sales just fell the hardest they have in over a year.

July's retail data came in ugly. The New York Times calls it the biggest drop in more than a year, with households running out of patience for persistent inflation. This is not a wobble in one category. It is the point where price rises stop being absorbed and start being refused.

The supply side of the same squeeze arrived within hours. A new US report accuses dozens of countries of helping China route goods through lower-tariff nations to dodge Trump's levies, which is exactly the sort of finding that produces another round of duties. On this side of the Atlantic, Ofwat has provisionally cleared thirteen water companies to charge more, and the government is weighing a cut to the 2030 electric car target from 80% to 50% under pressure from manufacturers. Everyone is repricing at once.

Berkshire filed into that mood with a fresh bet on US housing plus larger stakes in Alphabet and Delta. Buy the roof over people's heads and the infrastructure of the AI trade. Skip the middle.

What it means for your day Volume is coming out of the basket, so brand teams will defend share with promotion before they defend margin with price. That is the moment a cheaper route to shelf gets a proper hearing, which is the ShelfBuddy pitch in one sentence. For Tolani decks: any 2027 plan with a flat volume assumption needs a downside line under it this week, not in October. For Lucky Goods Club: low-ticket impulse categories are the last thing shoppers drop and the first thing they trade down within. Price the range accordingly before the autumn wholesale push.

Cryptography · The quiet layer

Google says private AI is now practical. A cryptographer says everything is about to go dark.

Filed from blog.google and Cryptography Engineering, 14 August 2026

Google published an account of how it is making private AI practical using homomorphic encryption, and it climbed to 198 points with 127 comments. The idea is old and the engineering is not. Homomorphic encryption lets a server compute on data it cannot read, returning an answer it never understood. For the best part of two decades that was a beautiful theorem that ran far too slowly to sell.

Hours later, Matthew Green's blog went up under the title "Everything is about to go dark", which is not the sort of headline that cryptographer writes for fun. The two posts pull in opposite directions on the same afternoon. The maths layer is getting more private while the policy layer argues about whether it should be allowed to.

The commercial read matters more than the politics. "We never see your data" has been marketing language for years, the sort of claim nobody could verify and everybody printed. If computing on encrypted data becomes routine, it turns into a technical claim with a yes or no answer. That is a very different conversation to have with a client's legal team.

Where this lands on your desk Any FMCG client running shopper data through an AI tool will be asked, within a year, whether the vendor can technically see the data or merely promises not to. Most vendors will not have an answer. Being the consultant who asks the question first, in plain language, is worth more than being the one who can explain the maths.

A PBS station may have lost 50TB because a storage vendor stopped answering.

Ars Technica reports a PBS station fears losing fifty terabytes of archive after being ghosted by its cloud storage provider. Iron Mountain told Ars it does not have access to the data on the hardware and servers in question. Nobody was hacked. Nobody was careless. A commercial relationship simply went quiet, and with it went thirty years of tape.

The security wire was busy too. A screen-sharing vulnerability that hands attackers full control of Macs is under active exploitation, letting a remote attacker log in with no password at all. Patch anything Apple-shaped on your network before Monday, including the family machines.

The pattern behind both stories is the same and it is not really about technology. Your data is yours until the counterparty decides otherwise. OneDrive, Supabase, Netlify, GitHub, Apps Script: every one of them is a company that could change terms, get acquired, or stop replying to email.

Weird science · Perception

Birds have been wearing ultraviolet the whole time. We simply cannot see it.

Somebody has built a gallery of birds photographed in ultraviolet, and the results are quietly astonishing. Plumage that reads as plain brown to us turns out to carry markings, patterns and signals in a band our eyes were never built for. The birds have been having an entire conversation in a colour we do not have a word for.

Elsewhere in the unseen: India's solar mission has returned findings on the corona problem, the long-standing embarrassment of the Sun's outer atmosphere running millions of degrees hotter than its surface with no agreed explanation. And a study out of Oulu links coffee consumption to metabolic health and sex hormones, which is either excellent news or a very good reason to stop reading studies about coffee.

Filed under the same heading, because they are the same idea. The instrument decides what counts as reality. Change the instrument, and things that were always there show up.

10.
The most quietly useful post on Hacker News today, 123 points and 84 comments, gives a name to the thing that has been happening to your market for three years:

"The TEMU-fication of software, digital goods and services."

For You

The argument runs like this. When the marginal cost of producing a digital thing falls towards zero, the market fills with items that are technically the product and functionally not. Cheap, plausible, endless. The buyer cannot tell the difference until after paying, and by then the cheap version has already reset what they think the thing is worth.

The evidence was sitting three slots up the same front page. DeepSeek published peak and off-peak pricing, 236 points and 124 comments. Inference is now sold like electricity, cheaper when nobody is using it. That is not a pricing experiment, that is what a fully commoditised input looks like once the novelty has worn off.

Marketing services are further along this road than most people running one will admit. Anyone can now produce a competent-looking deck, a plausible strategy document, a full campaign of content, for close to nothing. What they cannot produce is provenance and judgement: seventeen years of watching FMCG launches fail and knowing precisely which ones will, which no model has lived through because it was never in the room.

So the shape of the business is already decided. Charge properly for the judgement at the top. Ship the product at the bottom, where ShelfBuddy does the work once and sells it many times. Everything in the middle, competent execution billed by the hour, is being priced to the floor by somebody with an off-peak discount and no overheads. Do not compete on the middle. There is nothing there to win.
